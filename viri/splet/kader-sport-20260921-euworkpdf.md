EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
In 2024, the Expert Group on Health Systems Performance Assessment (HSPA EG) identified 
health workforce as a strategic priority, highlighting that appropriate staffing levels are 
fundamental to resilient, safe, and high-performing health systems. The choice of this topic 
builds on discussions with the HSPA EG around existing EU health workforce actions and is 
intended to complement them. 
The HSPA EG, supported by the European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies, 
undertook an in-depth analysis of how EU Member States and Norway define, implement, 
and use staffing levels. This report draws on three main sources of evidence: Member State 
responses to two surveys (system and sectoral levels), the October 2025 workshop on Staffing 
Levels for Healthcare, and a Policy Focus Group held in November 2025.
Health systems perform best when the right number and mix of health professionals are 
available in the right places and at the right time. Adequate staffing is directly linked to quality, 
safety, access, and staff well-being. While the evidence base supporting these links is strong, 
countries differ markedly in how staffing levels are defined, implemented, and monitored, 
reflecting variation in population needs, healthcare organisation, workforce composition, and 
available technology.
Countries adopt diverse approaches, ranging from fixed ratios to flexible models and hybrid 
systems. Fixed ratios offer predictability and transparency, often anchored in law or collective 
agreements, while flexible models allow responsiveness to demographic change, local demand, 
and technological development. Many systems now seek to balance these through adaptive 
frameworks that combine minimum thresholds with data-driven planning and workload-
based methodologies. Across the EU, there is growing momentum toward linking staffing 
levels with forecasting and planning tools that anticipate future workforce needs and service 
demand.
The survey revealed that staffing policies are primarily driven by health system goals, 
improving quality, safety, and workforce protection, rather than broader societal objectives 
such as economic growth or environmental sustainability. However, ensuring safe staffing also 
supports wider social and economic stability, especially in underserved or remote regions.
At the sectoral level, hospitals remain the main area where staffing ratios are applied, 
reflecting their structured environments and measurable workloads. Safety and effectiveness 
are the dominant goals. In primary care, fewer countries define ratios, instead adopting 
flexible, multidisciplinary, and regionally tailored approaches to address shortages and support 
integrated community care. Ambulatory and specialist care rely on adaptable, demand-based 
models that can respond to local needs and service diversity.
Governance of staffing levels is typically shared among multiple actors: ministries of health 
lead, but ministries of finance and education, professional bodies, health insurers, and social 
partners all play crucial roles. This multilevel collaboration ensures alignment between staffing, 
training, and financing. Increasingly, countries employ analytical tools such as population and 
workload profiling, forecasting, and skill-mix modelling to inform decisions and evaluate their 
effects. Where staffing levels are legally binding, they are often monitored through licensing, 
payer contracts, or inspections. Where they are non-binding, they still function as reference 
standards guiding planning, budgeting, and workforce allocation.

Links to health workforce planning, investment, and training are increasingly evident. Many 
Member States use staffing rules as a lever to adjust medical and nursing education capacity, 
steer financial allocation, and anticipate future shortages. This alignment between staffing 
and planning enhances policy coherence and system resilience.
Overall, the report identifies a clear shift away from rigid rules toward adaptive, evidence-
based staffing frameworks. These approaches combine minimum protections with flexibility 
through task-shifting, evolving competencies, regional tailoring, and continuous evaluation. 
While EU-wide staffing benchmarks are neither feasible nor desirable given system diversity, 
exchange of practices and cooperation on methodologies, indicators, and evaluation tools can 
strengthen comparability, learning, and accountability.
Staffing levels are not merely an operational parameter but a strategic policy lever linking 
workforce availability with health system performance. Using them effectively requires 
balancing assurance with adaptability, grounded in robust data, transparent governance, and 
a shared commitment to quality, safety, and workforce well-being across Europe’s health 
systems.

KEY MESSAGES
•	 Health system priorities drive staffing policies.
	 Countries define staffing levels primarily to improve patient safety, access to care, 
and workforce well-being. Broader societal goals such as economic development or 
sustainability tend to play a secondary role. A supported and adequately staffed health 
workforce remains fundamental to achieving strong population health outcomes.
•	 Technological, demographic, and epidemiological differences shape staffing needs.
	 For many countries, rigid or uniform ratios risk being impractical and incompatible with 
national and local systems due to variations in population health, service organisation, 
composition of health workforce and available technology.
•	 Staffing standards should be adaptable and responsive to various contexts.
	 Staffing levels should be defined by and responsive to each country’s health needs, 
organisational structure, and financial capacity rather than imposed through a one size-
fits-all approach at EU level.
•	 Fixed staffing ratios are less commonly applied in primary care, which requires flexible, 
multidisciplinary staffing approaches.
	 In the context of workforce shortages, diverse professional roles, and regional variation 
in capacity, fixed ratios can reinforce existing constraints. While patient-capitated, case-
mix–adjusted ratios could theoretically enable expansion, most systems rely instead on 
flexible, multidisciplinary team models. These models allow roles and workloads to be 
adjusted according to local workforce availability and population needs, making primary 
care particularly unsuited to rigid staffing norms.
•	 Nursing roles and staffing standards vary across contexts.
	 Differences in roles, competencies, and responsibilities mean that staffing levels are 
defined and applied differently across nursing professions and systems.
•	 Governance is shared across multiple actors.
	 Although Ministries of Health usually lead on staffing policies, governance is complex. 
Finance and education ministries, health insurers, professional bodies, and social partners 
all co-shape staffing decisions, balancing service needs with budgetary constraints, training 
capacity and workforce expectations.
•	 Health workforce planning is an underused lever for staffing policy.
	 Many countries already use workforce planning and forecasting to inform staffing levels, 
but these tools could be scaled up and used more strategically. Better forecasting of 
healthcare demand can generate more reliable estimates of workforce requirements, 
support innovative care models and strengthen staffing in community and primary care 
settings while underscoring the need for adaptable staffing approaches.
•	 Adaptive approaches are replacing rigid rules.
	 Countries are increasingly combining minimum standards with flexible safeguards to make 
staffing policies more responsive and sustainable. Examples include task-shifting, evolving 
competencies, regional tailoring, and periodic review mechanisms that allow systems to 
adjust over time.

•	 Evaluating staffing levels is essential to understand whether they deliver their 
intended impact.
	 Regular assessment is critical to determine whether staffing standards improve quality 
of care, enhance efficiency, support workforce retention, and strengthen overall health 
system performance.
•	 EU-wide benchmarks are not feasible, but shared learning through comparable

Introduction
Background of the study 
In 2014, the European Commission established the Expert Group on Health Systems 
Performance Assessment (1) (HSPA EG) to provide European Union countries with a dedicated 
forum to exchange experiences and to support national policymakers by identifying tools and 
methodologies to develop HSPA.
Each Member State can appoint up to two representatives as members of the Expert Group, 
and the European Commission is a member too. Norway and international organisations such 
as the World Health Organisation (WHO), the European Observatory on Health Systems and 
Policies and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), participate 
as observers, actively contributing to the work of the Group.
The HSPA EG defines priority areas that it wishes to work on. Since July 2022, the mandate 
of the Expert Group on HSPA has been expanded beyond HSPA, providing EU countries with 
a forum to discuss and promote strategic innovative approaches to strengthening health 
systems.
Within this expansion, healthcare workforce, with a focus on staffing levels, was selected as 
the priority topic for 2025. This focus prompted dedicated discussions and presentations on 
EU level actions addressing health workforce challenges. To support this work, the Expert 
Group agreed to conduct a survey on existing staffing levels and their impact on policies 
aimed at addressing health workforce shortages, to organise a dedicated workshop and to 
produce an HSPA EG report on this topic. The European Observatory on Health Systems and 
Policies was invited to support this work.
Accordingly, this report is informed by three complementary sources of qualitative evidence: i) 
the analysis of HSPA EG Members’ replies to the two questionnaires (discussed further below) (2), 
ii) the outcome of a workshop on Staffing Levels for Healthcare that was held on 16 October 
2025 in Brussels, and iii) the feedback received from a Policy Focus Group. The conclusions 
from the Policy Focus Group are reported in Annex 1.
High-performing health systems rely on having the right number and mix of health workers in 
place to achieve health system goals and objectives, such as those related to health outcomes, 
staff and patient satisfaction, access, and quality. Shortages or imbalances in staffing can 
lead to delays, unsafe treatments, and poor user experiences, especially in ambulatory care 
clinics, emergency departments, and primary care settings to name a few. In the frame of 
Health System Performance Assessments (HSPA), staffing levels can be used as a set of 
representative measures to link workforce availability to broader health system objectives. 
International research consistently shows that staffing levels are closely linked to a range of 
health system outcomes-including patient safety, staff wellbeing, access to care, and overall 
quality.
Staffing ratio policies have been implemented for longer in countries beyond the EU countries, 
providing a wider and more mature evidence base to inform their evaluation. A study by Sun 
et al., (2024) found that higher clinician staffing ratios in US health centres were associated 
with improved performance across multiple quality indicators, including vaccination rates,

Evidence on California’s mandated nurse-to-patient ratios further shows an association

Beyond the United States, Australia and New Zealand have established structured, data-

legislate nurse-to-patient ratios in 2000, later strengthened through the Safe Patient Care Act 
2015 (Government of Victoria, 2015), with similar frameworks introduced in Queensland and 
New South Wales (NSW Health, 2022). New Zealand’s Care Capacity Demand Management 
(CCDM) programme provides a national, evidence-based model linking patient acuity, workload 
measurement, and staffing forecasts that ensures consistency and quality while maintaining 
flexibility at the local level.
In the United Kingdom, staffing levels are embedded in professional and regulatory standards 
that support transparency and system accountability. The National Institute for Health and 
Care Excellence (NICE) provides evidence-based guidance on safe staffing (NICE, 2014).
This report presents the overview of staffing level solutions in the EU countries and Norway. 
Some of them offer examples of how staffing ratios have been evaluated. Stepping up efforts 
in health workforce planning, supported by the Joint Action HEROES (HEalth woRkfOrce to 
meet health challEngeS) (3), is an opportunity to advance and refine staffing level solutions, 
ensuring they are evidence-based, better aligned with service needs, and more resilient to 
future workforce pressures.
As countries embark on Health System Performance Assessments (HSPA) it would be beneficial 
to identify one or several indicators that help systems understand the link between workforce 
availability and broader health system objectives and highlight areas that require further 
investigation. These indicators can serve as a bridge between performance monitoring and 
health workforce planning, ensuring that staffing levels are not only tracked but also aligned 
with future service delivery needs. For instance, in Czechia’s HSPA framework, indicators 
explicitly refer to “future workforce needs”, thereby embedding workforce planning into 
the performance assessment process. This approach demonstrates how HSPA can provide 
actionable intelligence: it links data on current staffing levels with projections of demand, 
creating a structured mechanism to anticipate gaps and guide policy responses. By doing 
so, countries can strengthen the connection between assessing health system performance, 
planning for the workforce of tomorrow, and ensuring that staffing levels remain responsive 
to evolving health priorities.
Currently, many European countries have or are seeking to define and implement staffing 
standards across various sectors of their health systems. However, the terminologies and 
approaches used to establish these standards may differ between (and within) countries and 
there is little systematic knowledge about what countries are doing or about the emerging 
trends in staffing-related reforms.

Aim and structure
This analysis is informed by a two-questionnaire survey, one on system level staffing levels 
and the other on sectoral (hospital, primary care, ambulatory care, etc.) staffing levels. The aim 
of the survey is to map approaches and methods to address staffing levels in the healthcare 
workforce (HWF) in EU Member States and in Norway. By understanding how staffing levels are 
defined (Box 1), implemented, utilised and evaluated across countries, there is an opportunity 
to facilitate mutual learning and identify best practices. 
The study aims to answer the following questions:
•	 Do countries use staffing levels in their health systems (either at the system level or at the 
sectoral level)?
•	 How are staffing levels governed (from definition to implementation and then monitoring)?
•	 What do we know about the evaluation of staffing levels?
•	 How do regulations and guidelines on staffing levels link to HWF planning, investment and 
training capacities?
The remainder is organised as follows. After the methodology section, the results are presented 
in five chapters: Overview; The objectives and goals when defining staffing levels; Types of 
staffing levels in place; Governance; Evaluations and future outlook. Finally, the conclusions 
address implementation and policy transfer.
BOX 1- Definitions of different approaches to defining staffing levels
Countries adopt different approaches to defining staffing levels:
Fixed ratio staffing levels usually predefined ratios, typically specifying the number of health 
workers or professionals relative to patients, population groups, hospital beds, geographical 
areas. These ratios may be legally mandated or outlined in recommendations or guidelines.
Flexible staffing models are defined based on a combination of supply and demand factors 
aligning health worker availability with the healthcare needs of the population. This approach 
offers flexibility to adjust to workforce dynamics and evolving needs.
Other countries have no defined staffing levels. In some countries staffing levels are not 
formally defined, and neither fixed ratios nor flexible staffing models are used.
Minimum threshold is the lowest number of healthcare workers that is deemed necessary to 
maintain safe, effective, and sustainable service delivery. This can include thresholds in the 
form of aspirational targets, guiding principles and mandatory measures.
Methodology
Between February and June 2025, the European Observatory, the HSPA Expert Group and 
the HSPA Secretariat, designed, piloted, and distributed two questionnaires to 27 EU Member 
States and Norway, 28 in total (Figure 1). The questionnaires were developed following several 
rounds with the HSPA working group on healthcare workforce. It was emphasised that the 
questionnaires should map different healthcare sectors (e.g. hospital, primary care, etc.) and 
distinguish between doctors and nurses, as well as consider other professions.

Limitations
One of the limitations of the survey and thus this analysis is that some countries did not fill in 
any sectoral questionnaires. This could be due to several reasons: a) they did not have time, b) 
there were no experts available in the time given to fill out the questionnaire, c) there was no 
interest in filling out the questionnaire, d) they had put sectoral information into the system 
questionnaire and did not want to duplicate information, d) there was nothing to report on any 
sectoral level, or e) the sectoral questionnaires might be incomplete in the sense that some 
Member States might have focused their response on hospitals and in doing so might have 
skipped filling out a sectoral questionnaire for ambulatory care even though there might have 
been something relevant to report.  
It should also be acknowledged that, beyond differences in staffing levels and professional 
classifications, the definition, scope and boundaries of health sectors themselves may vary 
across countries. Structural differences in how countries organise and categorise hospital, 
ambulatory, specialist or primary care services may affect how responses were interpreted and 
reported. These variations limit the direct comparability of findings related to distinct health 
system sectors and should be taken into account when drawing policy-relevant conclusions.
It must also be noted that the HSPA Expert Group agreed on a scope that did not include long 
term care (LTC). This was done primarily because for many countries LTC area rests within 
the social ministries and not within the Ministry of Health, and therefore outside the scope of 
this research.

Chapter 2: What are Member States’ objectives and 
goals when defining staffing levels?
System level
The eight countries that stated they defined staffing levels at the system level were asked 
their reasons for doing so (6). The options suggested as reasons can be found in Box 2. 
BOX 2 Definitions of declared objectives and goals for defining staffing levels
1. Health System–Related Goals
Countries want staffing approaches to help the health system perform better and be more 
resilient. These goals typically include people-centeredness, health improvement, and financial 
protection and they are the outcomes used to measure the performance of a health system. 
They are very relevant to patients and citizens. The health workforce influences these final 
goals by impacting the intermediate objectives.
Effectiveness of care: The ability of an intervention to have a meaningful effect on 
patients in normal clinical conditions.
Safety of care: The prevention of errors and adverse effects associated with health 
services.
User experience: Users’ beliefs, preferences, perceptions, responses, and behaviours 
that occur before, during and after.
Access to care: The opportunity or ease with which individuals or communities are able 
to use appropriate health services.
Patient centeredness: As a goal which captures how far the health system adequately 
addresses people’s non-medical health needs.
Health improvement: A fundamental goal of any health system. It encompasses the 
health of the population at different points in the life cycle and is measured through 
morbidity and mortality indicators as well as aggregate life expectancy.
Financial protection: Refers here to the health system’s role in protecting the population 
against risk. For example, if there are not enough doctors working in the public sector, 
people may be forced to visit a doctor in the private sector, leading to out-of-pocket 
spending.
Quality of care: is used as a generic term comprising various dimensions including e.g. 
access, safety, effectiveness, user experience but also efficiency and equity.

How the model works
Czechia employs a fixed staffing model anchored in its national health strategy Health 2030. 
The central target is set at 32 health professionals per 1,000 inhabitants by 2030, covering all 
major professional groups such as doctors, nurses, and other healthcare staff. This aspirational

specialty plus one nurse, while in primary care, similar rules apply with additional standards

are also covered by sector-specific rules under the same directive.

Why they use a fixed model
The fixed ratio approach in Czechia is driven by several factors. Firstly, clarity and simplicity: 
fixed ratios are straightforward to measure, track, and communicate, both to policymakers 
and to the public. This makes them politically and administratively practical. Secondly, they 
support comparability across time and with other countries, as the aspirational target was

Goals behind the model
Czechia’s staffing ratios are designed to serve multiple objectives. Health system goals are 
mostly access and quality, while safety is the main goal in hospitals. Staffing ratios aim to

future workforce planning with population needs.

Czechia’s fixed ratio staffing model combines a clear national target (32 per 1,000) with 
detailed operational rules for specific care settings. Its main strengths lie in simplicity,

prioritises both patient safety and workforce sustainability, while serving as a long-term

BOX 4 - Ireland uses flexible staffing to align the health workforce with system needs
How the model works
Ireland uses a flexible model for defining staffing levels at the system level, managed primarily 
through the Health Service Executive (HSE) Pay and Numbers Strategy (PNS). This strategy 
is an annual publication derived from the national Budget and the HSE’s National Service 
Plan. It sets out the number and allocation of staff that the HSE, the national public health 
provider, has approval and funding for in a given year. Staffing ceilings are defined in Whole 
Time Equivalent (WTE) terms, which provide a standardised way to account for staff numbers 
across different professions. 
The process involves several actors. Staffing ceilings are agreed between the Department 
of Health (D/Health) and the Department of Public Expenditure, National Development Plan 
Delivery and Reform (DPENDPDR). Once the overall ceiling is set, responsibility for deciding 
which posts will be filled lies jointly with D/Health and the HSE. In addition, the Department of 
Children, Disability, and Equality (DCDE) is responsible for funding staff working in disability 
services, reflecting the system’s integration of health and social care.
Why they use a flexible model
Ireland adopted this approach to strengthen pay bill management, improve oversight, and 
increase governance transparency. Unlike rigid fixed staffing ratios, the PNS allows the system 
to adapt workforce numbers annually based on budgetary realities, service needs, and evolving 
health priorities. It provides the government with a high degree of control over expenditure 
while enabling the HSE to align staff numbers with operational and population health needs.

BOX 5 - Malta’s evidence-based workforce planning approach
Malta has chosen not to introduce binding staffing ratios or formal minimum thresholds. 
Authorities in the Public Health Service were concerned that setting such requirements could 
disrupt service provision due to limited workforce supply and create risks of industrial unrest, 
given the highly unionised nature of the health sector. Instead, staffing decisions are made 
based on need, allowing flexibility and responsiveness to immediate service demands.
What it does instead
Rather than relying on fixed ratios, Malta has embraced a data-driven, evidence-based 
workforce planning approach. The shift began with WHO-supported consultations in 2019–
2020, which laid the groundwork for sustainable health workforce forecasting. In 2022, the 
Ministry for Health and Active Ageing launched the Health Workforce Strategy (2022–2030),

The Maltese approach combines international collaboration (EU and WHO), advanced tools,

adaptable to changing healthcare needs.

Box 6 - Norway opts for quality over quotas
Norway does not mandate specific staffing ratios across its health and care system. Instead, 
it uses a quality-based governance model where each service provider must ensure their care 
meets the defined standard of “soundness.” This allows:
•	 Local discretion in staffing decisions.
•	 Adaptation to patient complexity and service type.
•	 Avoidance of rigid staffing formulas that may not suit all contexts.
This very flexible approach to staffing levels in Norway works well and is necessary for several 
reasons. First, hospitals and care providers in Norway vary widely in the populations that are 
served (densely populated cities like Oslo versus remote villages). In addition, patient needs, 
i.e. acute care versus long-term care also vary. In sum, it is believed that local providers 
know best how to staff appropriately for their given situation and circumstances. Secondly, 
Norway’s healthcare system is decentralised. The country has 357 municipalities for primary

cities. This type of decentralisation requires a certain amount of flexibility for continuous 
and effective care across the very diverse settings. Thirdly, Norway already anticipates 
future workforce shortages and thus wants to keep the flexibility and agility of its workforce 
organisation in order to more easily be able to task shift or restructure roles, thereby better 
being able to adjust teams dynamically based on real-time needs.
Summary
Norway deliberately chooses not to set staffing levels and this decision is rooted in its 
decentralised health system, geographic diversity, and desire for future adaptability. By 
empowering local providers and focusing on service quality rather than numbers, Norway 
seeks to maintain high standards while adapting to demographic and workforce challenges.

investment. Each region determines its workforce needs according to population characteristics 
and demand, while healthcare centres often develop their own staff planning models within 
regional regulations. At the national level, the Ministry of Health contributes by publishing 
periodic reports projecting the needs of doctors and nurses (e.g., Oferta y necesidad de médicos 
especialistas en España 2023–2035 and Necesidades Profesionales de Enfermería 2024). 
These reports are not binding but provide a shared evidence base for workforce planning 
across the country.
Non-binding staffing framework
Because staffing levels are not legally binding at either the national or regional level, Spain’s 
link between staffing, investment, and training is more flexible but also more fragmented. 
Regions include staffing-related investments in their annual budgets, and workforce expansion 
is financed at the community level. This allows each region to tailor investment decisions to 
local needs, such as addressing rural shortages or boosting nursing capacity. At the same time, 
the absence of mandatory national staffing thresholds makes it harder to ensure uniformity 
and equity across regions. Some communities may respond quickly to shortages by expanding 
hiring or investing in training capacity, while others do not.
The link to training and workforce planning
Training and workforce planning are connected through national and regional coordination 
mechanisms. The NHS Human Resources Commission brings together the Ministry of Health, 
autonomous communities, and other ministries (education, universities, defence, justice) 
to align training capacity with projected staffing needs. This collaboration helps determine 
the number of medical and nursing students admitted each year and ensures that training 
expansion supports long-term workforce planning. Yet, because the benchmarks are indicative 
rather than prescriptive, success depends on how actively each region engages with the 
recommendations.

Are staffing levels legally binding?
In several countries, staffing levels are legally binding and anchored in legislation or regulatory 
decrees. Out of the nine countries that define staffing levels at the system level, five have 
staffing levels that are legally binding (Bulgaria, Hungary, Poland, Greece and Czechia). In 
Bulgaria, staffing levels are set by regulatory requirements and legally binding contracts

the NHIF and the Executive Agency for Medical Supervision enforcing compliance (Box 14). 
Hungary enforces minimum staffing requirements through legislation such as the 2003 Decree 
on healthcare services, making compliance obligatory at licensing and during inspections. 
Poland mandates binding staffing norms through Ministry of Health ordinances and additional 
executive regulations in hospitals, particularly for nurses, linking them directly to provider 
contracts with the National Health Fund (Box 16). Greece also embeds staffing requirements 
in law and presidential decrees, specifying hospital staffing by specialty and monitoring 
recruitment through centrally approved posts. In Czechia, the overarching national staffing 
target serves as a non-binding benchmark reviewed biennially; however, the specific staffing 
ratios in the health directive are legally binding and enforced. These binding rules establish a 
floor for safe care and ensure sufficient staffing is a condition for service provision.
Compliance mechanisms vary but are typically monitored by state authorities, payers, or 
supervisory agencies
In Hungary, authorities verify adherence to staffing thresholds during licensing and operational 
inspections, checking both qualifications and numbers (Box 20). Poland’s National Health Fund 
monitors compliance as part of its contracting process, ensuring providers meet workforce 
requirements for reimbursement. In Greece, oversight is shared between the Ministry of 
Health and Regional Health Authorities, supported by digital reporting systems such as the 
BI-Health platform. Bulgaria combines legal obligations with social partnership agreements 
and compliance is monitored by both the National Health Insurance Fund and the Executive 
Agency for Medical Supervision (Box 14). These systems highlight the use of strong regulatory 
tools backed by contractual enforcement or inspections to secure compliance.

Italy enforces staffing levels indirectly through strict expenditure caps, monitored jointly by

technical committee (Tavolo tecnico per gli adempimenti), which reviews whether regions 
respect budgetary ceilings tied to essential care delivery. While this does not prescribe 
ratios at the clinical level, it legally constrains workforce growth and makes staffing subject 
to financial accountability (Box 13). Similarly, in Bulgaria, staffing levels are embedded in 
contractual agreements tied to financing negotiations, making compliance enforceable via 
funding channels. These two examples demonstrate how legal binding can be embedded not 
only in ratios but also in financial ceilings and reimbursement structures.

Sectoral level: hospitals, primary care and other sectors
What are the governance solutions for staffing levels, how are they agreed upon and who 
is involved?
In the hospital sector, governance solutions are typically anchored in legal frameworks, 
planning tools, and negotiated agreements. In Slovakia, Greece and Hungary, Ministries of 
Health establish legally binding minimum thresholds, often tied to licensing and compliance 
inspections. Bulgaria, Belgium, and Luxembourg rely on a mix of legislation and negotiated 
frameworks with insurance funds or professional bodies, where compliance is monitored 
and sanctions or loss of funding may follow non-compliance (Box 22). Austria and Germany 
integrate staffing rules into broader hospital legislation and financing frameworks, including 
DRG-based payment systems, thereby making noncompliance with staffing requirements a 
condition for a reduced reimbursement (Box 23). While this approach constrains the flexibility 
originally embedded in DRGs, it reflects a deliberate policy choice to balance efficiency 
incentives with safeguards for quality of care, patient safety, and workforce sustainability. 
These arrangements are agreed at national level by central ministries, health insurers, and 
social partners, ensuring consistency across the system.
In primary care, governance is generally more decentralised and flexible. Slovakia and Spain 
use modelling and regional evaluations of provider networks, with regional authorities 
deciding whether to follow national recommendations (Box 24). Portugal sets staffing levels 
through strategic planning bodies like the ACSS, but these are mainly guidelines rather than 
binding rules (Box 12). Austria embeds primary care staffing in structural plans or quotas 
negotiated between health insurance funds, governments, and professional associations. In 
Bulgaria, staffing levels are legally binding, tied to organograms and national frameworks, with 
ministries and regional health authorities overseeing allocation. Agreement in primary care 
often involves negotiations between ministries of health, education, insurers, and professional 
chambers to balance supply, demand, and access.
In other sectors such as ambulatory, specialist, nursing, and dental care, governance mixes 
central rules with regional flexibility. Slovakia applies fixed but adaptable formulas for 
ambulatory care, while Austria sets national frameworks that regions or providers cannot 
undercut but may supplement. Spain uses reference values, modelling, and performance 
contracts rather than enforceable thresholds, leaving room for regional adaptation for 
specialists. Across these non-hospital sectors, solutions are agreed through joint commissions, 
advisory bodies, and regional health authorities, involving governments, insurers, professional 
bodies, and providers, ensuring staffing rules reflect both system-level goals and local realities. 
Norway highlights how governance differs in a Nordic model where trust-based, decentralised 
planning plays a bigger role. In hospitals, Norway sets staffing expectations mainly through 
national legislation, collective agreements, and professional standards, but implementation is 
delegated to regional health authorities and individual hospitals, which adapt to local needs. 
In primary care, municipalities have responsibility for staffing, guided by national frameworks 
and financial transfers, with professional associations (e.g., the Norwegian Nurses Organisation, 
the Medical Association) involved in shaping guidelines and good practice norms. Agreements 
are therefore less about hard thresholds and more about negotiated standards, capacity 
planning, and ensuring that investments and training keep pace with demographic and care 
needs.

BOX 22 - Governance of staffing levels in Belgian hospitals
How it works
The governance of hospital staffing levels in Belgium is shared between the federal government 
and the regions. The federal government sets the minimum legal requirements for staffing 
through Royal Decrees, which define fixed ratios for wards, units, and care programs. These 
ratios act as accreditation standards: if a hospital fails to comply, it cannot receive authorisation 
to operate that service and loses the associated funding. The regions are responsible for 
inspection and enforcement. They verify compliance with the federal standards and, in theory, 
may revoke a hospital’s authorisation if ratios are not met. Moreover, regions can set stricter 
requirements beyond the federal minimums, creating room for variation across the country.
Multi-level stakeholders
The governance process incorporates the perspectives of multiple actors. The hospital sector 
is formally consulted through an advisory committee, ensuring that providers’ views are taken 
into account. In some cases, the regions are also included in discussions when developing 
or adjusting standards, reflecting Belgium’s federal structure. This shared approach aims to 
balance uniform national requirements with the ability to address regional and local needs.
Legal minimum
Although the standards are designed as minimal thresholds, Belgian hospitals typically employ 
more staff than the legal minimum. One of the reasons lies in the financing arrangements: the 
federal government not only sets the rules concerning the legal minimum staffing but also 
funds hospitals. 
To supplement the minimum staffing levels, the federal budget subsidises more staff than 
required by the Royal Decrees. In addition, hospitals can voluntarily choose to recruit more 
personnel in some services. This creates a gap between the legal framework and the reality: 
while compliance with the minimum is mandatory, the actual workforce composition usually 
exceeds it.
Hospitals that fail to uphold the staffing ratios theoretically risk losing authorisation and 
funding. In practice, enforcement varies depending on regional policy priorities. Nonetheless, 
the binding nature of the Royal Decrees and the role of the regions in the inspection procedures 
ensure that staffing standards remain central to hospital governance.
Summary
Belgium’s governance of hospital staffing levels combines federal standard-setting, regional 
enforcement, and hospital sector consultation. This layered governance structure reflects 
Belgium’s federal complexity while safeguarding patient safety and service quality.

BOX 23 - Germany’s governance model for hospital staffing levels
How it works
Governance solutions for hospital staffing levels in Germany are structured around a dual 
regulatory approach, combining federal legislation with delegated decision-making. For 
somatic hospitals, staffing levels are set directly through a regulation issued by the Federal 
Ministry of Health (BMG), which establishes binding minimum staffing requirements across key 
hospital units. These thresholds are based on empirical workload research, targeting wards 
with particularly high patient demand. For psychiatric units, responsibility is delegated to 
the Federal Joint Committee (G-BA), the country’s highest self-governing body in healthcare, 
which uses acuity-based assessments to determine staffing needs. This division ensures that 
both somatic and psychiatric care are governed through evidence-based rules adapted to 
their distinct service realities.
Multiple stakeholders
The process of agreeing on staffing levels involves multiple actors. The BMG sets binding 
requirements for somatic care after consultation with research experts and professional 
stakeholders, while the G-BA develops psychiatric standards through a structured negotiation 
process. The G-BA is composed of representatives from statutory health insurers, hospitals, 
and physicians’ associations, with patient representatives holding advisory roles. This inclusive 
structure ensures that staffing regulations reflect not only federal policy priorities but also 
professional standards, payer perspectives, and patient needs. Research institutions provide 
the data underpinning staffing norms, while professional associations ensure alignment with 
clinical practice.
Compliance
Compliance with staffing rules is enforced through reporting and sanctions. Hospitals must 
report their staffing levels and the number of understaffed shifts quarterly and annually. 
If monthly averages fall below mandated thresholds, financial sanctions are applied under 
agreements linked to the German Diagnosis-Related Groups (G-DRG) system. These rules 
are non-negotiable at the local level—hospitals may exceed minimums but cannot go below 
them, except under extraordinary circumstances (i.e. COVID-19 pandemic). This centralised, 
legally binding model secures baseline staffing, while still allowing hospitals to invest in higher 
staffing ratios if desired. 
Summary
Germany’s governance of hospitals combines federal authority, corporatist negotiation, 
and strict enforcement, making staffing regulations a cornerstone of hospital workforce 
management.

System level
Do countries see opportunities and risks of setting staffing levels at the system level?
Across all surveyed countries, regardless of whether they define staffing levels or not, most 
see opportunities in defining staffing levels, mainly to improve safety, quality, and equity, and 
they are building the data and methods to do it more efficiently (See Table 2). Slovenia, for 
example, plans to introduce a unified, workload-based methodology and develop national 
standards in 2026 (Box 26). Slovakia is updating rules to cap patients per nurse/doctor and is 
mapping regional minimum/optimal thresholds in ambulatory care. Spain is piloting a patient-
acuity classification, ‘Safe Nurse Staffing’, with EU technical support to align nursing numbers 
with care needs. It intends to apply the Safe Nurse Staffing classification across hospital, 
primary, emergency (and long-term) care. Hungary is designing a dual framework that 
combines workload distribution with operational minimums for both inpatient and outpatient 
services. Similar planning tools and strategies are advancing in Portugal through the Joint 
Action HEROES and their 2030 HRH plan, in Estonia where they are in the process of building 
a HWF forecasting model for primary care (Box 25), in Latvia where they are creating a 
2025–2029 workforce strategy and a unified register, and in Malta and Greece through a 
planning tool and a single data repository. Romania is developing a multi-year HRH strategy 
and working on capacity building, Poland is mapping health needs and expanding education 
pathways, Czechia is reviewing its staffing directive, and Bulgaria is developing a national 
monitoring methodology. 
The risks most often cited in relation to staffing levels include their rigidity, weak data, and 
misalignment with real-world workload or geography. Norway, for example, explicitly argues 
that rigid staffing levels can work against flexibility, hence its emphasis on task-shifting (Box 
6), guideline updates and cross-trained education pathways. Sweden’s focus on a National 
Health Competence Council and a primary-care-led transformation also emphasises skills 
and organisation over fixed numbers (Box 8). Slovakia cited that its existing rulebook (an 
older ministerial regulation specifying how staffing levels for doctors and nurses should be 
calculated) is outdated while Slovenia’s Blue Books of Staffing Levels for doctors and nurses 
exist but were not adopted because the original methodology could not be verified (Box 
26). Latvia still needs to operationalise its model inside ministry systems and Hungary is 
updating minimum requirements to avoid having to rely on obsolete headcounts. Poland’s 
heavy education and migration levers are helpful but require constant calibration and Greece 
highlights persistent shortages in remote areas even with the incentives it offers. Taken 
together, it seems that countries value staffing levels for safety and transparency but tend 
to caution that poorly measured or overly rigid rules can freeze inefficiencies and worsen 
shortages.
In general, there appears to be a momentum toward defining staffing levels or equivalent 
guardrails, but the direction seems to be more data-driven and conditional rather than 
purely ratio-based. The emerging consensus appears to lean towards anchoring staffing 
in measurable workload, patient acuity, and risk adjustment. All of which is supported by 
forecasting models, unified registers, and better governance, while preserving flexibility 
for task-shifting and new care models. Countries give the impression of seeing the 
opportunity to raise quality and transparency and to target shortages more precisely, 
but they also appear to be aware of the risk that strict mandates could reduce efficiency 
or hinder innovation. Therefore, some countries are pairing the minima they do set with 
adaptive tools, regional tailoring, and ongoing evaluation.

Do countries evaluate their staffing levels at the system level?
Health workforce planning can effectively serve as a bridge for evaluating staffing levels in 
healthcare by providing a structured, evidence-based framework for assessing whether the 
number, mix, and distribution of personnel align with population health needs and service 
demand. Several countries highlight the role of the Joint Action HEROES in improving models 
and data and the potential of this work to inform staffing decisions. Through the use of 
forecasting models, workload analysis, and skill-mix assessment, workforce planning enables 
organisations to move beyond simple headcounts and systematically determine staffing 
adequacy, shortages, or surpluses. It also links staffing evaluation to broader health system 
objectives - including quality of care, patient safety, access, and financial sustainability - 
ensuring that staffing decisions support strategic priorities rather than isolated operational 
concerns. Moreover, because workforce planning is iterative, it supports continuous monitoring 
and adjustment, allowing healthcare systems to respond to shifts in demographics, disease 
patterns, and service utilisation. As a result, health workforce planning functions not only 
as a tool for long-term policy formulation but also as a practical mechanism for informing 
day-to-day staffing decisions, thereby bridging the gap between system-level planning and 
organisational workforce deployment.
Many countries that define staffing levels have developed formal mechanisms to evaluate 
staffing levels by combining data collection, forecasting, and long-term workforce strategies. 
Poland, for example, has a legally mandated “map of health needs” that is updated annually 
with demographic, epidemiological, and workforce analyses (Box 27). This feeds directly into 
both national and provincial transformation plans, ensuring workforce planning is embedded 
in broader health system reforms. Hungary has also established strong structures, with the 
National Hospital Directorate General and the National Centre for Public Health and Pharmacy 
jointly producing reports and forecasts on trends such as retirement, migration, and supply 
gaps. Romania uses a relatively institutionalised and data-driven approach that links staffing 
evaluation to national strategies and regional planning (Box 28). Spain, although it does not 
define staffing levels at the system level, also integrates workforce planning into a national 
strategy aligned with the WHO-Europe framework, while also experimenting with patient 
acuity–based staffing models for nurses. Other countries, such as Ireland and Greece, rely on 
indicators, tools, strategies to align workforce supply with population needs, highlighting a 
clear commitment to defining and evaluating staffing levels at the system scale.
Several countries adopt a lighter or more experimental approach to evaluation. Bulgaria, 
for example, has opted for a less formal, expert-driven model of evaluation without a 
standardised methodology (Box 31). Latvia has piloted a forecasting and mapping model 
with EU support, but its routine integration into health planning is still pending. Slovenia has 
produced the Blue Book of Staffing Levels for doctors and nurses, which sets out minimum 
requirements, but these standards remain unofficial because the methodology has not been 
fully validated (Box 26). Italy has only recently introduced an experimental methodology 
through the 2023 Ministerial Decree that has been applied in several regions but remains 
limited to hospital and home care. Estonia is in the process of developing a forecasting model 
that will cover primary care by 2025 (Box 25), while Sweden has created a National Health 
Competence Council to coordinate planning across regions and institutions. Portugal does 
not currently have a strategy for assessing staffing levels but does have a few mechanisms 
in place that allow for some level of assessment. These include regular national workforce 
statistics compiled by health authorities, workforce data in budget and planning documents, 
internal organisational plans of health units, and regional needs assessments using indicators 
like provider-to-population ratios, waiting times, and coverage levels to allow a partial and 
indirect assessment of staffing adequacy. These cases show countries building capacity 
for system-level evaluation, although with varying degrees of formalisation and national 
coverage.

BOX 27 - A legally anchored system-level evaluation approach in Poland
How they evaluate
Poland evaluates health workforce staffing needs through a statutory Map of Health Needs 
led by the Ministry of Health under the 2004 Act on health services financed from public 
funds. The map is refreshed annually and combines demographic and epidemiological 
analysis with a stock-and-flow view of resources, i.e. facilities, services and the healthcare 
workforce, to identify national and provincial priorities. Findings are then entered into a 
National Transformation Plan, while each province (voivodeship) prepares a matching 
provincial transformation plan. Voivodes (regional governors), together with the NFZ (National 
Health Fund) branches and founders of medical entities, also conduct ongoing situational 
assessments to address emerging gaps.
The annual cycle draws on the latest available datasets and emphasises use and performance, 
not only headcounts. It reviews utilisation of services, capacity, and observed bottlenecks and 
then recommends actions by geography and care area. This creates a consistent, repeatable 
feedback loop between evidence (maps), planning (national/provincial plans), and operational 
adjustments (regional stakeholder reviews). Because the instrument is embedded in law, 
updates to priorities and actions are routinised rather than ad-hoc.
Evaluation linked directly to education and training
Poland ties its system evaluation directly to supply pipelines. Each year the Ministry of Health sets 
admission limits for medical and dental faculties and allocates residency (specialty) positions, 
considering university capacity (staffing, equipment, premises) and mapped population needs. 
Recent reforms strengthened pre-graduate training standards (from 2024/25) to increase 
practical exposure, communication skills, and cancer-care competencies. In 2020, they also 
modernised the post-graduate pathways to align with Directive 2005/36/EC. To accelerate 
entry, medical and dental students no longer have to wait until after graduation to take their 
final licensing exam, and trainees may take the national specialty exam in their final training 
year.

BOX 29 - Evaluating Safe Nurse Staffing in Ireland
Ireland’s Framework for Safe Nurse Staffing and Skill Mix was evaluated through a 
comprehensive pilot implemented across six wards in three hospitals. The setting for the 
research was all medical, surgical and specialist wards in the three pilot hospitals (excluding 
intensive care units, emergency and outpatients’ departments. The central objective was to 
determine whether evidence-based staffing levels improved patient outcomes, strengthened 
the workforce, and generated economic value. The evaluation included clinical, organisational, 
workforce, and a full economic analysis.
Quantifying the costs of implementation
Introducing safe staffing required both a direct investment, i.e. hiring staff, and the conversion 
of agency staff to substantive posts, i.e. shifting staff from costly agency contracts into 
permanent or directly employed positions. The pilot identified the need for 21.2 whole-time 
equivalents (WTE) through new staffing investment alongside 15.1 WTE through agency 
conversion, producing a total requirement of 36.3 WTE across the pilot wards. The average 
investment per ward was approximately €160,000.

Initially, introducing safe staffing generated net additional costs because permanent staff

The framework produced substantial, measurable improvements in both patient and staff

The pilot showed that a structured methodology for determining safe nursing staffing levels

and increasing efficiency, particularly through reduced agency use. These findings supported 
national rollout and demonstrated that safe staffing functions as an “invest to save” model 
that strengthens care quality and long-term sustainability. This example also stresses that 
a pilot can be an important first step in evaluating the feasibility, effectiveness and impacts 
of an intervention before its wider implementation in a specific context of the health system.
BOX 30 - Evaluating minimum nurse staffing regulations in Germany
Germany introduced a mandatory system of minimum nurse-to-patient staffing ratios across 
hospital settings, aiming to improve safety and reduce understaffing in high-risk units. Since 
2019, these legally binding ratios apply to “nursing-intensive units,” including surgical wards, 
paediatrics, cardiology, neurology, and intensive care, covering 93.5% of all hospitalisation 
days. Non-compliance leads to financial sanctions, creating strong incentives for hospitals to 
meet the regulated thresholds. Examples include a 2.5:1 day-shift ratio in adult ICUs and 10:1 
in internal medicine during daytime hours.
Evaluation approach
The system was formally evaluated by the National Association of Statutory Health Insurance 
Funds and the German Hospital Association. The 2024 evaluation covered 1,300 hospitals, 
analysing (1) compliance data and (2) interviews with Chief Nursing Officers to assess 
operational and workforce effects.
Quantitative and qualitative findings
The data analysis found that 15% of all shifts between 2021 and 2023 were understaffed. The 
most affected areas were special paediatrics (27%) and stroke units (19.9%), while general 
paediatrics (10.4%) and internal medicine (≈11%) had the lowest levels of understaffing. 
Understaffing occurred at similar rates across day and night shifts.

BOX 31 - Expert-based and emerging approaches to staffing level evaluations in Bulgaria
How they evaluate now
Staffing level evaluations in Bulgaria are led primarily by the Ministry of Health together 
with the National Centre for Public Health and Analysis. Their role is to monitor and assess 
the availability of health personnel, paying particular attention to those specialised fields 
where shortages are most acute. Professional organisations, such as medical and nursing 
associations, also contribute their expertise and ensure that the evaluations reflect the 
realities of clinical practice. However, there is currently no standardised methodology for this 
assessment. Instead, evaluations are carried out largely at an expert-opinion level, relying 
on the knowledge and judgment of specialists rather than systematic or data-driven models.
What they plan to change
This ad-hoc and unregulated approach has led to inconsistencies in staffing assessments 
across different institutions and actors. Discrepancies between evaluations often make 
it difficult to establish a shared and reliable picture of workforce needs. Policymakers and 
stakeholders have acknowledged that this fragmented system undermines the capacity to 
plan effectively for shortages and imbalances in the health workforce. Recognising these 
limitations, the Bulgarian government has committed to developing a formal methodology 
for monitoring and evaluating its healthcare personnel. This reform is embedded within the 
country’s Recovery and Resilience Plan, adopted by the Council of Ministers, which sets out a 
roadmap for building a more structured and evidence-based system of workforce planning.
Summary
By moving from expert-driven monitoring to structured methodologies, Bulgaria aims 
to improve accuracy, comparability, and reliability in its staffing assessments, aligning its 
workforce planning with broader European efforts toward evidence-based health system 
governance.

outdated, inconsistent, or unable to respond to changing workforce and patient needs.

BOX 32 - Ireland’s research-based framework for safe nurse staffing and skill mix
What is it
Ireland has developed a very research-driven approach to hospital staffing in Europe through its 
Framework for Safe Nurse Staffing and Skill Mix. The Framework was created via a multi-year 
program of research, with independent evaluations carried out throughout its development. 
These evaluations demonstrated significant improvements in patient safety, quality of care, 
and staff well-being when the model was applied. Based on this evidence, the Framework was 
adopted as national policy in 2020, covering adult medical and surgical care settings, with 
implementation expanding to emergency care. The model is structured around nursing hours 
per patient, allowing ratios to be adapted to the complexity and acuity of patients across 
specialist areas, including critical care. While considered a fixed staffing approach for nurses, 
it has built-in flexibility, with different ratios set by care area to better reflect patient needs.
Why does it work so well
Staffing models aligned with patient needs tend to lead not only to better health outcomes 
but also to increased retention and improved job satisfaction for nurses. The Frameworks 
objectives explicitly include ensuring effectiveness, patient-centredness, safety of care, and 
protection of health workers’ well-being. Its implementation is supported by system-level 
modelling approaches that project aggregated demand and supply across the public, voluntary, 
and private sectors, while also considering population change, healthy ageing, prevention 
policies, and task-sharing reforms. This shift away from density benchmarks towards a needs-
based modelling approach positions Ireland as a frontrunner in linking workforce planning to 
future demographic and epidemiological realities.
Where do the opportunities lie
Ireland will extend the Framework to long-term residential care for older people and 
community/primary care. The model is being independently re-evaluated to test its ongoing 
effectiveness, while compliance monitoring systems are being developed to ensure consistent 
implementation nationwide. Though the Framework is not yet solidified by legislation, it 
is embedded in the national policy and represents a hybrid approach: fixed staffing ratios 
informed by rigorous research but applied flexibly across care settings. This strategy offers 
both predictability and adaptability, linking day-to-day staffing practices with broader 
workforce planning and training pipelines.
Summary
Ireland’s Framework for Safe Nurse Staffing and Skill Mix uses a research-based, needs-driven 
model that improves patient safety and nurse well-being while guiding national workforce 
planning.

BOX 33 - Austria’s structural plan for primary care staffing is a balance between flexibility

What is it
Austria evaluates and plans staffing levels in primary care through the Austrian Structural 
Plan for Healthcare (ÖSG), which serves as the national framework for the entire public health

analysis, epidemiological data, and accessibility thresholds into planning. The ÖSG and RSGs

medical supply and demand across both inpatient and outpatient care. Updated annually, the 
model not only maps the current workforce but also forecasts retirements, training needs, and 
likely shifts between sectors. These insights are meant to guide decision-making on training 
places, redistribution of professionals, and adaptation of competencies.

ensuring national consistency. By linking staffing levels to both health outcomes and workforce 
sustainability, Austria seeks to balance effectiveness, equity of access, quality of care, and 
staff well-being.

Austria’s Structural Plan for Healthcare combines fixed staffing ratios with flexible

BOX 34 - Evaluating hospital staffing levels in Greece
How they evaluate
Greece runs a centrally coordinated, indicator-based process of evaluation for hospitals. Each 
year the Ministry of the Interior and Ministry of Finance set the total number of posts that 
can be announced. Public hospitals can then submit requests to Regional Health Authorities 
(RHAs). Using operational indicators, the Ministry of Health allocates approved posts across 
RHAs and hospitals. Doctors can also be hired as auxiliary or contracted staff (typically for 12–
24 months) to allow for flexibility between recruitment rounds. Nursing and other personnel 
are recruited permanently via the Supreme Council for Civil Personnel Selection, with the 
Ministry of Health allocating posts as they are needed.
What evidence is used
Allocation decisions consider a common set of service-pressure metrics: beds, bed occupancy, 
number of clinics, days of hospitalisation, hospitalisation rates, surgeries per year, and other 
operational indicators. These are combined with population profiling, workload/service-use 
data, local workforce profiling (distribution gaps), and modelling of organisational constraints 
(i.e. approved organograms, expected retirements). The approach is flexible by design thereby 
aims to best reflect Greece’s geography, especially the islands and the mountainous areas, 
where access and recruitment challenges require tailored responses.
How do they consider the dispersed geography
To staff remote or underserved areas, Greece provides monthly financial incentives, relocation 
and housing support, faster career progression, extra education leave, and other non-financial 
benefits. Bonuses are targeted at specialties such as internal medicine, family medicine, 
anaesthesiology. Compliance is monitored via regular reporting and audits (vacant/filled posts, 
re-advertisements), BI-Health (Business intelligence for health) submissions from hospitals to 
RHAs, and a central auxiliary personnel registry within the Ministry of Health.
Digitalisation and continued improvement
A new Human Resources Information System (HRIS), a part of the Quality for All / HEALTH-
IQ program, consolidates data and indicators to track both quantitative staffing and skill-
mix. The first operable version was planned for October 2025, and the full delivery will be 
effective for February 2026. Together with legally binding post limits and indicator-driven 
allocation, this would tighten the feedback loop between needs, recruitment, incentives, and 
regional distribution, allowing Greece to keep hospital staffing aligned with effectiveness, 
safety, access, workforce well-being, and retention goals.
Summary
Greece uses an indicator-driven, centrally coordinated process to allocate hospital staff, 
combining operational metrics with geographic incentives and a new HRIS to align recruitment 
with system needs and workforce sustainability.

What they are changing
While minimum ratios currently dominate, Germany is transitioning towards workload-
based evaluation tools. At the same time, a federal regulation from July 2024 requires 
hospitals to submit data that will underpin a new nurse staffing measurement system 
(Pflegepersonalbemessungsverordnung, PPBV), moving away from rigid ratios towards more 
adaptive, workload-driven planning.
Why it is important
Germany’s model balances legal minimum guarantees with a gradual shift toward evidence-
based planning. Current ratios ensure a transparent floor that cannot be undercut, making 
risks of understaffing highly visible and sanctionable. However, the system does not yet allow 
flexible adjustment to local hospital realities, except in extraordinary cases like pandemics. 
Germany is moving from compliance-driven evaluation toward a dynamic, data-informed 
approach that seeks to align staffing with patient needs, staff well-being, and system 
sustainability.
Summary
Germany combines legally mandated minimum staffing ratios with new workload-based 
evaluation tools, moving from compliance-driven regulation toward a more flexible, evidence-
based approach to hospital staffing.
BOX 36 - Hungary’s centralised evaluation of primary care staffing
How they evaluate primary care
The evaluation of primary care (PC) staffing is centralised and statute-based. Two national 
bodies lead it: the National Hospital Directorate General (OKFŐ) and the National Centre 
for Public Health and Pharmacy (NNGYK). Under the Health Act, the Minister responsible for 
Health (currently the Minister of Interior) sets sectoral policy, defines minimum professional 
conditions for service provision, and issues the detailed rules that govern provider organisation 
and operation. OKFŐ also acts as the organiser for family medicine districts (“praxiskezelő”), 
establishing practice areas and ensuring equal territorial access.
OKFŐ operates a sectoral HR monitoring system that integrates operational records of health 
workers, payroll and staffing statistics, PC workforce data (GPs, paediatric GPs, dentists, 
nurses/health visitors), and indicators of outward mobility. Regular reports, trend analyses, 
and forecasts are produced, i.e., expected nurse exits due to ageing, or the impact of wage 
measures. Planning combines population profiling (demographics and geography), workload 
and utilisation data (service use, local demand), workforce profiling (distribution gaps), and 
modelling of organisational constraints (approved posts, infrastructure). Minimum catchment 
thresholds anchor the service design: at least 600 residents for paediatric GP districts and 
1,200 residents for adult/mixed GP districts, with adjustments for local characteristics to 
guarantee access and efficiency.

Conclusions: What considerations do you have to think 
about when implementing or changing staffing levels?
Implementing or changing staffing levels is not simply a technical exercise, rather it is a policy 
decision that requires balancing evidence, flexibility, and governance. While staffing ratios can 
seem like a clear-cut solution, the reality is that their effectiveness depends on context, the 
methods used to define them, and how they are embedded in a country’s wider health system 
goals and health workforce policies.
Ratios are not transferable, but methodologies are
A key consideration is that numbers themselves are not transferable across countries; 
however, the methodologies behind them are. Ratios that work in one country may be 
unsuitable in another due to differences in workforce composition, service delivery models, 
or financing structures. What can be shared are the approaches for determining those ratios, 
i.e. the analytical methods that underpin them. Tools that calculate workload, assess patient 
acuity, or model workforce demand can be adapted to different systems and used to support 
international learning without reproducing raw figures or inappropriate benchmarks. 
Existing staffing models can be adapted, they do not have to be replaced
Staffing models do not always need to start from scratch. Long-standing frameworks can 
evolve through evidence-based review rather than being completely replaced. Continuous 
evaluation and adjustment are key to ensuring that staffing levels remain fit for purpose, 
realistic and credible. This is important because they are then better able to reflect the changes 
that are likely to arise in service delivery, patient needs, and workforce capacity.
Not defining ratios still means someone needs to make a decision
A system without defined staffing levels does not mean staffing decisions disappear, it simply 
means they are made flexibly without clearly identifying decision makers. In such systems, 
decisions tend to be made at the local, institutional or managerial level. This can work well 
in some contexts but may increase the risk of inconsistency and inequity across facilities or 
regions. In effect, a lack of a formal definition simply transfers decision-making power to a 
less transparent or less regulated level.
Different levels of governance play distinct roles
Governance is another critical dimension. In many countries, staffing levels are determined 
at the regional or local levels, with the national authorities focusing on workforce planning, 
forecasting, and strategic coordination. This division of roles allows flexibility close to 
service delivery while maintaining alignment with national objectives. It also highlights the 
importance of coherence between the levels: if national forecasting is disconnected from 
local implementation, mismatches between supply, demand, and funding can quickly emerge.
However, not all countries define staffing levels explicitly, and thus there is often a fear that 
doing so might reduce the quality or flexibility of care. Some governments and professional 
bodies often worry that fixed ratios could be interpreted as maximums rather than minimums, 
leading to rigid application and a potential decline in care standards. Addressing these 
concerns requires adaptive approaches that pair defined minimums with mechanisms that 
preserve professional judgment and allow for contextual adjustments.

Bureaucracy can stifle useful reforms
Overly complex reporting requirements or slow approval processes can delay implementation 
and discourage innovation. Streamlining governance processes and focusing on practical, 
evidence-based evaluation is essential to make staffing reforms effective rather than 
burdensome. Simplified yet transparent accountability mechanisms enable systems to evolve 
while maintaining oversight.
In the same vein, extensive stakeholder consultations can slow down reforms. In some countries, 
governments must consult a wide range of stakeholders before deciding on staffing levels 
or releasing data for international comparison. While broad consultation is critical to ensure 
ownership and legitimacy of staffing policies, overly complex or lengthy stakeholder processes 
can hinder timely decision-making. Streamlined, transparent engagement mechanisms can 
help maintain momentum while preserving trust.
Staffing reforms must connect health system goals with health workforce 
goals 
Effective implementation ultimately requires a clear link between health system goals 
and health workforce goals. Concentrating only on ones without the others risks creating 
distortions: a well-staffed system that fails to meet population health needs, or a well-
designed service model with too few staff to deliver it. Evaluating staffing reforms through 
both lenses ensures that workforce capacity supports system performance, and that system 
design makes efficient and sustainable use of human resources. 
In summary, successful practices with staffing ratios are those that are context-sensitive, 
evidence-informed, and grounded in continuous evaluation. They rely less on copying 
ratios and more on adapting methodologies, clarifying governance, and integrating 
workforce and system objectives. The challenge is to create frameworks that are both 
structured and flexible _ providing assurance where it is needed, but leaving space for 
adaptation, innovation, and learning across countries.

INTRODUCTION 
Background to the questionnaire 
High-performing health systems rely on having the right number and mix of health workers in place to achieve health 
system goals and objectives, such as those related to health outcomes, staff and patient satisfaction, access, and 
quality. Shortages or imbalances in stafﬁng can lead to delays, unsafe treatments, and poor user experiences, 
especially in ambulatory care clinics, emergency departments, and primary care settings to name a few. In the frame 
of Health System Performance Assessments (HSPA), stafﬁng levels can be used as a set of representative measures 
to link workforce availability to broader health system objectives (i.e. access to healthcare, quality and safety of care, 
and population health, to name a few).   
As countries embark on Health System Performance Assessments (HSPA) it would be beneﬁcial to identify one or 
several indicators that help systems understand the link between workforce availability and broader health system 
objectives, and highlight areas that require further investigation.  
Currently, many European countries have or are seeking to deﬁne and implement stafﬁng standards across various 
sectors of their health systems. However, the terminologies and approaches used to establish these standards may 
differ between (and within) countries and there is little systematic knowledge about what countries are doing or about 
the emerging trends in stafﬁng-related reforms. 
The HSPA Expert Group identiﬁed health workforce as a priority topic for 2024-2025. After discussions and 
presentations on the EU actions addressing health workforce, the Expert Group decided at the October 2024 plenary 
to carry out a survey on existing practices regarding stafﬁng levels, and their impact on the policies addressing health 
workforce shortages, to organise a workshop and issue a conference report. At the request of the European 
Commission, the European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies, an observer in the Expert Group was 
requested to support the work.  
Survey aim 
The aim of this survey is to therefore map approaches and methods to address stafﬁng levels in the healthcare 
workforce in EU Member States and in Norway. By understanding how stafﬁng levels are deﬁned, implemented, utilized 
and evaluated across countries, there is an opportunity to facilitate mutual learning and identify best practice cases. 
The survey has been developed following several rounds of consultation with the HSPA co-chairs and members of 
the HSPA working group on the healthcare workforce. It was emphasized that the survey should map different 
healthcare sectors (e.g. hospital, primary care, etc.) and distinguish between doctors and nurses, as well as consider 
other professions.

Health system objectives and goals: they include people-centeredness, health improvement, and ﬁnancial 
protection. These goals are the outcomes used to measure the performance of a health system. They are very 
relevant to patients and citizens. The health workforce influences these ﬁnal goals by impacting the intermediate 
objectives. 
Effectiveness of care: The ability of an intervention to have a meaningful effect on patients in normal clinical 
conditions. 
Safety of care: The prevention of errors and adverse effects associated with health services.  
User experience: Users’ beliefs, preferences, perceptions, responses, and behaviors that occur before, during and 
after. 
Access to care: The opportunity or ease with which individuals or communities are able to use appropriate health 
services. 
Patient centeredness: As a goal which captures how far the health system adequately addresses people’s  
non-medical health needs. 
Health improvement: A fundamental goal of any health system. It encompasses the health of the population at 
different points in the life cycle and is measured through morbidity and mortality indicators as well as aggregate 
life expectancy. 
Financial protection: Refers here to the health system’s role in protecting the population against risk. For 
example, if there are not enough doctors working in the public sector, people may be forced to visit a doctor in the 
private sector, leading to out-of-pocket spending. 
Quality of care: is used as a generic term comprising various dimensions including e.g. access, safety, 
effectiveness, user experience but also efﬁciency and equity. 
Goals related to health workers: These goals focus on safeguarding the well-being of health workers, ensuring their 
job satisfaction, and creating a supportive work environment that enables them to thrive in their roles. 
Protecting the health and well-being of health workers: adequate stafﬁng level are a key to ensure that health 
workers are not exposed to constant work overload affecting their health. 
Improving retention by improving working conditions: adequate stafﬁng levels are an instrument to retain health 
workers in healthcare organizations. 
Societal Goals: These goals are outside the direct scope of the health system but are still closely linked to stafﬁng 
levels. For example, the environmental impact of health workers' green skills can contribute to sustainability. A well-
staffed health workforce can also positively affect economic development by supporting a healthier population, 
which in turn contributes to a stronger economy. Secure stafﬁng levels during times of economic crisis have been 
shown to help maintain economic stability. Additionally, stafﬁng levels can affect social cohesion, especially in areas 
with limited access to care, such as medical deserts or deprived urban areas. 
Social cohesion: to achieve collective commitment to unity, mutual support, and shared responsibility among 
individuals and communities. This goal encompasses eliminating discrimination, reducing income inequality, and 
ensuring equal access to healthcare.  
Economic development: is a vital societal goal which enables higher living standards, job creation, and increased 
innovation. 
Environmental sustainability: refers to the interconnectedness of environmental concerns with broader 
sustainability objectives. It refers to a balanced and inclusive approach to economic growth that seeks to meet 
the needs of present and future generations without compromising natural resources and global ecosystems. 
 
NOTE Intermediate Objectives:  
These objectives are directly related to how well health systems deliver services and achieve overall health outcomes.  
They include effectiveness, safety, user experience, and access to care. Achieving these objectives is crucial for high-quality 
service delivery. For this to happen, health systems need the right number of skilled workers in the right places at the right 
times. These intermediate objectives are a key link between stafﬁng levels and the ﬁnal health system goals. If intermediate 
objectives aren’t met, it’s unlikely the system will perform well in terms of people-centered care, health improvement, and 
ﬁnancial protection. The relationship between stafﬁng levels and health system outcomes can be understood through these 
intermediate objectives.

INTRODUCTION 
Background to the questionnaire 
High-performing health systems rely on having the right number and mix of health workers in place to achieve health 
system goals and objectives, such as those related to health outcomes, staff and patient satisfaction, access, and 
quality. Shortages or imbalances in stafﬁng can lead to delays, unsafe treatments, and poor user experiences, 
especially in ambulatory care clinics, emergency departments, and primary care settings to name a few. In the frame 
of Health System Performance Assessments (HSPA), stafﬁng levels can be used as a set of representative measures 
to link workforce availability to broader health system objectives (i.e. access to healthcare, quality and safety of care, 
health outcomes and population health, to name a few).   
As countries embark on a Health System Performance Assessments (HSPA) it would be beneﬁcial to identify one or 
several indicators that help systems understand the link between workforce availability and broader health system 
objectives, and highlight areas that require further investigation.  
Currently, many European countries have or are seeking to deﬁne and implement stafﬁng standards across various 
sectors of their health systems. However, the terminologies and approaches used to establish these standards may 
differ between (and within) countries and there is little systematic knowledge about what countries are doing or about 
the emerging trends in stafﬁng-related reforms. 
The HSPA Expert Group identiﬁed health workforce as a priority topic for 2024-2025. After discussions and 
presentations on the EU actions addressing health workforce, the Expert Group decided at the October 2024 plenary 
to carry out a survey on existing practices regarding stafﬁng levels, and their impact on the policies addressing health 
workforce shortages, to organise a workshop and issue a conference report. At the request of the European 
Commission, the European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies, an observer in the Expert Group was 
requested to support the work.  
Survey aim 
The aim of this survey is to therefore map approaches and methods to address stafﬁng levels in the healthcare 
workforce in EU Member States and in Norway. By understanding how stafﬁng levels are deﬁned, implemented, utilized 
and evaluated across countries, there is an opportunity to facilitate mutual learning and identify best practice cases. 
The survey has been developed following several rounds of consultation with the HSPA co-chairs and members of 
the HSPA working group on the healthcare workforce. It was emphasized that the survey should map different 
healthcare sectors (e.g. hospital, primary care, etc.) and distinguish between doctors and nurses, as well as consider 
other  professions.
